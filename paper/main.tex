\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Isobenefit Urbanism proposes settlements in which every home lies
within a walk of a mixed-use centre and of green space. The published
model grows such settlements on an abstract plain, where a few rules
guarantee the walks while the urban form emerges. Real places arrive
with existing towns, fragmented green space, rivers, railways,
motorways, and steep slopes, and with the transit around which policy
already organises growth. This paper adapts the model to real terrain
and implements it as an open-source QGIS plugin, so that planners
can sketch scenarios that support discussion and deliberation at the
early stages of planning. The
plugin prepares a scenario from OpenStreetMap and elevation data, or
from the user's own layers, keeps existing settlements as they stand,
and simulates growth within the land released for development. The
planner steers the outcome through the scenario settings: the walking
distances, the population target for new residents, the density tiers
and their shares, the land withheld from development, and the
existing or proposed transit that growth leans toward. The result
is a set of plan options written as GIS layers, in which new homes
keep a mixed-use centre and green space within the specified
walking distances. The pipeline is demonstrated on three
hypothetical cases: a new settlement at Cambourne, a green-belt
release at Crews Hill in London, and a hillside expansion at
Pajarito in Medell\'in, each sensitive to a different setting. The code, scenario data, and
parameters are open, and the results can be rebuilt from committed
sources at fixed seeds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Isobenefit Urbanism \citep{dacci2019} proposes that the benefits of a
settlement should be distributed evenly. Its prefix \emph{iso} means
equal: wherever one lives, a local centre with shops, services, and
workplaces should be within a walk, and so should green space.
\citet{dacci2013} introduced the concept in a futures study and later
formalised it as a cellular-automaton model of morphogenesis, published
with a Python simulator \citep{dacci_voto2023,voto_code}. The automaton
grows a grid of land cells from seeded centralities (the published
model's term for centres) under a small set of rules that maintain
the walking limits as the settlement grows. The approach sits between master planning and
laissez-faire growth, because a few simple rules guide development while
the detailed form emerges spontaneously \citep{dacci_banister_white2024}.

The published model runs on an abstract grid, in which land
starts as nature, distances are straight lines, and a single radius,
typically about a fifteen-minute walk, governs access both to
centres and to green space. The abstraction keeps the morphology of
the rules easy to study, but a real site is constrained in ways the
abstract grid is not. Part of the land is already built, and a plan
has to keep those settlements as they stand, while the remaining
green space is fragmented and partly protected. Rivers, railways,
motorways, and steep slopes block walking routes, so straight-line
distance overstates the access residents would have. The walkability
guarantee therefore does not carry over from the abstract grid, and
re-establishing it on a real site means fitting growth around the
existing fabric and measuring access as walks that detour around
the obstructions.

This paper operationalises Isobenefit Urbanism as a QGIS plugin for
the early stages of planning, when options are conceptualised and
residents and experts consulted, a role akin to that of
sketch-planning support systems \citep[cf.][]{klosterman1999,
geertman_stillwell2004, waddell2002}. The
plugin also supports transit-oriented development, since policy
already organises growth around stations and stops, and the premise
that growth and density belong within walking reach of transit
\citep{calthorpe1993, cervero_kockelman1997, ibraeva2020} overlaps
with the walkable-access tenet of Isobenefit Urbanism. The
quantities the model works from are settings a
planner supplies: how far people will walk, how many new residents
the scenario is for, at what densities, on which land, and around
which transit, and the growth rules then work within those
settings. The outputs are written as GIS layers.

The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item An adaptation of the published rules to real geography. The
  model keeps existing settlements and protected green space as they
  stand, simulates growth within land released for development, and
  measures access as walks that detour around barriers and terrain.
  Departures from the published model are tabulated
  (Table~\ref{tab:rules}).
\item An open, reproducible implementation: a QGIS plugin that wraps a
  simulation engine published on PyPI, with documentation and the
  case-study scenarios.
\item Enhancements beyond the published model: two walking distances
  in place of the single radius, one to a centre and one to green
  space; a graded preference toward transit, with hubs anchoring
  centres; centres founded by the population they serve rather than
  by a seeding probability; and post-processing that places the
  centres and grades the densities, keeping new homes within a walk
  of a centre.
\end{enumerate}

We demonstrate the pipeline on three case studies, each dominated by a
different constraint and steered through a different setting.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The published model}

The Isobenefit-cities simulator \citep{dacci_voto2023} belongs to a
long line of cellular models of urban form with real-world planning
applications \citep{white_engelen1993, clarke1997,
batty2005, sante2010}, and differs from models fitted to observed
growth in that its rules enforce a walkability guarantee while growth
proceeds. It grows settlements on a
square grid that starts as nature. A nature cell adjacent to built land may
become built on two conditions: a centrality must lie within the
radius T\(^*\), typically about one kilometre or a fifteen-minute
walk, and building the cell must not breach the green-space rules,
which keep nature as one connected region whose width does not fall
below T\(^*\). An eligible cell builds by a random draw at each
step, so the built edge advances irregularly. New
centralities seed by rarer draws, either beside built land that has
outgrown its centres or on open land. Each built block takes its
population density from one of three tiers, high, medium, or low,
drawn at set shares, and the run stops when the total population
reaches its cap.

\subsection{Walking distances and standards}

We replace the single radius with two walking distances, one to a
centre and one to green space. Both are set in the QGIS plugin, whose
defaults of 1{,}200~m for the centre walk and 400~m for the
green walk are approximately fifteen-minute and five-minute walks
respectively. The walkable-city literature generally treats
destinations within a ten-to-fifteen-minute walk as walkable
\citep{moreno2021, abbiasov2024}, and observed walking distances for
everyday errands are of the same order \citep{yang_diezroux2012}. For
green space, the WHO Regional Office for Europe advises everyday access
within about 300~m straight-line distance \citep{who2016}, and Natural
England's accessible natural greenspace standard uses the same figure
\citep{naturalengland2010}. Both standards also set a minimum size
for qualifying green space: the WHO advice concerns spaces of at
least half a hectare to one hectare, and the Natural England standard
applies from 2 hectares, which is the default for the
minimum park area (Section~\ref{sec:growth}). The 400~m default is slightly
longer than the advised 300~m because a walking route goes around
obstacles and covers more distance than the straight line.
Transit adds two further distances (Section~\ref{sec:transit}). The
stop catchment, 400~m by default, bounds the walk to a bus stop or a
drawn transit route; it matches the quarter-mile bus-stop catchment
conventional in transit-oriented development \citep{calthorpe1993,
ibraeva2020}, and observed walks to bus stops are of that order,
with an 85th-percentile walk of about 520~m in Montreal
\citep{elgeneidy2014}. The hub catchment, 1{,}200~m by default,
bounds the walk to a station or a designated hub. People walk
further to rail than to a bus stop \citep{daniels_mulley2013}:
station-area practice starts at half a mile, and the same Montreal
study observes an 85th-percentile walk to commuter rail of about
1{,}260~m. The default matches the centre walk, because a hub
anchors a centre.
Section~\ref{sec:cases} sweeps the centre walk across 800, 1{,}200,
and 1{,}600~m; the green walk stays at 400~m throughout.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The QGIS plugin comprises two tools, one for data preparation and one
for simulation. The preparation tool downloads a scenario's input
layers from OpenStreetMap. Users can instead supply equivalent
layers from their own data, or download the layers and edit them.
The simulation tool passes these inputs to the
engine, published on PyPI, which applies the growth rules and the
post-processing. The subsections below follow that sequence.

\subsection{Real inputs}
\label{sec:inputs}

Each scenario is defined by a simulation boundary in a metric coordinate
reference system and a set of vector layers. The case-study scenarios
fetch these layers from OpenStreetMap
\citep{osm} with the preparation tool. The layers cover existing
built fabric, protected green space, existing mixed-use centres,
unbuildable land (the areas treated as unavailable for development),
streets, the walkable ways (footpaths and streets a pedestrian may
use), public-transport stops, rail and tram stations,
railways, and industrial land, which counts as unbuildable for new
homes. Terrain slope is mapped from the Copernicus GLO-30 elevation model
\citep{copernicus_glo30} as bands steeper than 15, 20, 25, and 30
degrees. The bands are a separate layer that users can edit from
local knowledge.

\subsection{Grid preparation}
\label{sec:gridprep}

The simulation tool rasterises the input layers to a working grid at a
user-selected cell size, such as 50~m, though a finer grid may be
preferable where the extent and the available computation allow. Each
cell takes its class from the layer covering its centre:
existing built fabric, protected green space, or unbuildable land. A cell that
intersects none of the layers is marked open land. The tool then adds
to the unbuildable class in two ways: it carves corridors along
motorways, trunk and primary roads, railways, rivers, canals, and
streams, and it marks the slope
bands that lie above the scenario's slope limit. Street geometry the
walkable-ways layer does not carry is kept for display only.

The model treats new development and the existing fabric differently.
The existing fabric stays as mapped and is not redeveloped: the
input data rarely describe existing densities and services in
enough detail to model them, and the emphasis is on new
development. The population target and the walking distances
therefore concern new homes, and the constraints in the sections
that follow are derived from and applied to the new growth, except
the green-space rules, which also protect the parks the existing
fabric uses.

The simulation tool then screens the open land against the service
viability threshold, the population a minimal local centre needs;
the default of 2{,}000 people sits at the small end of published
facility catchments \citep{barton2021}. A region too small to hold
the threshold population is kept when it lies within the centre
walk of an existing centre, where new growth is infill served by
that centre. The rest, typically small islands and slivers of open
land enclosed by existing development or unbuildable corridors, is
excluded from new development before the run.

The growth rules and the reported metrics measure
distance with the bounded grid walk, computed by a Dijkstra search:
the shortest path over traversable cells, which detours around
unbuildable land and is cut off at the maximum walking distance. A diagonal step between two
blocked cells is refused, so a walk cannot pass through the corner
where two blocked cells meet. A corridor is crossable where a
way people can walk crosses it: the walkable-ways layer is applied
after the carving, and the
cells it touches stay closed to development though a walk may pass
through them. Without that, a carved corridor severs land a
pedestrian can reach, and a footbridge over a railway has no
effect.

\subsection{The growth rules}
\label{sec:growth}

A run proceeds in iterations, each applying the growth rules to
the land cells in a shuffled order to avoid the directional bias
of a fixed scan. A cell is a candidate
for building when it is empty, buildable, touches built land, and
lies within the centre walk of a centre.
New growth attaches only to built land that carries a centre, so
existing settlements without one, such as outlying farmsteads and
hamlets, receive no new growth. A run starts with the existing mixed-use
centres and with a centre seeded at each transit hub, a rail or tram
station or a point the planner designates, and development gathers
around the hubs from the first iteration.

Building a candidate must leave the green network intact. A park is
a contiguous green area of at least the minimum park area, 2
hectares by default, which follows Natural England's accessible
natural greenspace standard \citep{naturalengland2010}. New
development may not shrink a park below the minimum, whether by
splitting it or by building along its edge, and a green corridor
between two settlements may not narrow below a minimum span, 400~m
by default. Green
that one settlement encloses on its own, in the crook of a C or a U,
is a bay rather than a corridor, and the settlement may build into
it. A candidate must itself have a park within the green walk, and
new growth may not take the park a built cell depends on, the
existing fabric included: a cell that reaches a park within the green
walk must still reach one afterwards. Existing fabric that has no park
within the walk is left as it is, since the model does not redevelop
it.

A candidate that passes the checks builds by a random draw. The draw
staggers the built edge so that ensemble members differ; it paces
growth without shaping the plan, and is not a setting. Each built
cell draws one of the three density tiers
according to the shares, and a run stops when it reaches the
population target.

New centres are earned rather than drawn. Each new home is charged to
the nearest centre that provides for new development, measured by the
grid walk: one the run founded, or one pinned at a transit hub. An
existing centre serves the town it stands in and takes no charge. New
homes with no such centre within the walk accumulate in a single
unserved account instead. Infill near an existing centre is charged
this way too, though post-processing later serves it from that
centre rather than granting it one of its own. Once any account reaches the service
viability threshold, it releases a centre, and each further threshold
releases another, so the number of settlements follows the population
that justifies them rather than a probability.

A released centre is placed at the next cell the scan reaches that has
no provision within the centre walk, which is where post-processing
would otherwise have to add one. A centre is also released when the
land within reach is built out, which is what happens where terrain
confines development to less ground than the threshold needs; without
that release, a run would stop with its target unmet. One setting
governs where a released centre may go: attached to existing
development, or free to start a detached settlement.

Where a scenario supplies transit, the build draw above and the
placement of a released centre lean toward it, by an amount that fades
with the walk to the nearest stop or station
(Section~\ref{sec:transit}); with no transit supplied, both stand
as described.

\subsection{Ensembles and selection}
\label{sec:ensemble}

The growth rules are stochastic, so a single run is one pattern
among the many the same inputs allow. The tool therefore runs an
ensemble, fifty runs in the scenarios reported here. Each run
starts from the same inputs with its own random seed and grows a
complete settlement pattern that satisfies the constraints, so
runs build in different places. The likelihood layers
summarise this variation by recording, for each cell, the fraction
of runs in which it ended as built land or as green space, which indicates how
likely an area is to develop under the constraints.

Because the runs vary cell by cell, averaging them blends the
constraints away; the blended result no longer satisfies the walking
limits, so the layers are informational only. We instead select the
best single run, the one whose candidate plan from the
post-processing of Section~\ref{sec:fouroutputs} scores lowest.
When a run houses the target within the walks, its score is the
mean of two averages, its new homes' walk to a centre and their walk
to green space, weighted equally. Both enter in metres, so the centre
walk, having the longer limit, carries more of it. Two penalty terms apply otherwise: a home beyond
either walk enters the average at twice that walk's limit, and
each unhoused person of the target enters as a home beyond both
walks. A candidate keeps its homes within the centre walk by
construction, so the penalties bear on green access and on the
unhoused remainder, and a run cannot improve its score by housing
fewer people. The case studies that follow report the selected plan
alone.

The mean walks reported in this paper include no penalty terms;
each averages over the new homes that reach a centre or green
space within the walk.

\subsection{Post-processing and the four outputs}
\label{sec:fouroutputs}

Post-processing turns the best single run into the four outputs.
The first, the raw grown state, is the run exactly as it grew,
coloured by the density tiers it drew. The other three are plan
options: the same fabric and drawn densities under three centre
arrangements, and no post-processing edit may leave a new home
beyond the centre walk of a centre.

Viability is tested once, before the centres are arranged. Centres
are provisionally placed at the positions central to the new homes
they serve, and each must gather at least the threshold population
of new homes within the centre walk; one that falls short is
removed, growth that no surviving centre reaches reverts to nature,
and placement and test repeat on the reduced fabric until they are
stable. Existing centres, the hub centres, and the existing fabric
are untouched, and this pruning is the only step that removes
growth. The three plan options share the pruned fabric, so they
house the same population and differ only in where their centres
stand.

The centres are then arranged in one of three ways.
\emph{As grown} keeps the run's centres where they
seeded, adding one where new development is left without provision
within the walk, which happens where pruning removed a centre. \emph{Optimised placement} places each centre at the
position central to the new homes it serves, which shortens their
mean walk, and adds a centre wherever new development lacks
provision within the walk. \emph{Fewest centres} starts from the optimised
placement and removes centres one at a time, most redundant first,
for as long as every home keeps a centre within the walk: the
fewest centres the walk constraint permits. Infill too small to
meet the threshold is served by an existing centre within the walk
and receives no centre of its own. Optimised placement is the
selected plan; run selection scores each run's optimised-placement
candidate, and the case studies report that option.

The densities are arranged last, once the centres are final. Within
each settlement the tiers are dealt again from the shares and
assigned by the walk to the middle of the nearest centre, so density
is highest at the heart of a centre and falls away from it, through
the centre's own area and on into the fabric around it. Where a
centre stands at a settlement's edge, as a station against a rail
line does, density grades away from that edge. The shares, and with
them the population, are unchanged; only the arrangement changes.

A centre is a mixed-use district whose cells carry homes at the
arranged densities alongside floor area for services. Each centre
is sized by the new residents nearest to it, at 20~\sqm{} of
centre area per resident by default, and each resident counts
toward one centre. The
default covers the shops, services, and workplaces of
neighbourhood-centre standards \citep{barton2021}. Amenity provision
scales sublinearly with settlement size \citep{bettencourt2013}, so a
single ratio would misstate provision across a wide range of
settlement sizes; over the range this tool addresses, the implied
correction is small, and the ratio is a setting where a scenario needs
a different one.

An ensemble run loads its results into the QGIS project as one
layer group: the existing fabric for comparison,
the raw grown state, and the three plan options as categorical
rasters, each coloured by density tier with the centres in a
distinct hue and the centre count in the layer name. A further
layer maps the growth that pruning removed, and the two likelihood
layers complete the group. Alongside the layers the tool writes a
plain-text run report listing the parameters, the run summary,
the four outputs' metrics side by side, and the achieved shares
and centre audit, so the post-processing edits stay visible. A
single run, outside the ensemble, is written instead as a
temporal animation of the growth, one frame per iteration.

\subsection{Transit-oriented growth}
\label{sec:transit}

Growth leans toward the transit a scenario supplies. Each stop,
station, and corridor cell projects a pull that is strongest at the source and
fades to nothing at the edge of its catchment: 400~m for a stop or a
drawn route, 1{,}200~m for a station or a designated hub, both measured
by the grid walk, with the strongest pull taken where two overlap. The
development draws are scaled between the full rate where transit is
nearest and a fraction of it where transit does not reach, so
development gathers around transit by degree rather than changing
abruptly at a boundary. A scenario that supplies no transit layer is
unaffected: with no pull, the draws run at their ordinary rate.

Two settings govern the lean. The corridor preference sets how strongly
it applies, from 0, which ignores transit, to 1, which confines growth
to what transit reaches; the default of 0.35 favours transit-served land
without letting it dictate the plan. The two catchment distances set how
far the pull carries, and they differ because a station draws from
further away than a bus stop does.

A transit hub has one further role: it seeds a centre at the start of a
run, so development gathers around it, and post-processing pins that
centre, which is neither pruned nor repositioned and is sized like any
other. Stations enter the hubs layer with the scenario's other inputs; a
point added by hand designates a centre where the planner intends one,
such as a transit-oriented development around a planned station.

Whatever the settings, the run report gives the share of new homes
within the transit catchments and the mean walk to the nearest stop or
hub; these metrics do not enter run selection.

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}
\label{sec:software}

The implementation comprises a simulation engine and a QGIS plugin.
The engine is a Rust crate, published on PyPI as
\texttt{isobenefit}, whose small Python interface, built with PyO3,
accepts and returns arrays; the engine does not import QGIS. The
QGIS plugin \citep{qgis} supplies the data preparation, the run
dialog, and the post-processing and reporting, and depends only on
what QGIS already bundles plus the engine. The QGIS plugin repository does not permit
shipping binaries, so the compiled engine installs from PyPI and the
plugin offers to install it on first run.

Results are deterministic and independent of thread count, because
each stochastic draw derives from its own fixed seed and parallel
totals are integer sums whose order does not matter. The test suite
cross-checks the Rust engine against a reference Python
implementation of the bounded grid walk and of the labelling of
contiguous regions. A single verification script runs the same
checks as the continuous-integration pipeline (formatting, linting,
Rust and Python tests, and the website build); releases are tagged,
version-locked between engine and plugin, and published
automatically. The scenario inputs are committed, so the results
in this paper rebuild offline from source at fixed seeds. A fifty-run
ensemble on the grids of Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} completes in one to
six seconds on a laptop, and the post-processing that scores and
arranges the runs takes the larger share of a run's time.

\section{Case studies}
\label{sec:cases}

We ran the pipeline on three demonstration cases chosen for contrast
in the land they offer: a
free-standing new settlement (Cambourne, UK), a green-belt release at a
metropolitan edge (Crews Hill, London), and a topography-bound hillside
expansion (Pajarito, Medell\'in). Each case is most sensitive to a
different setting, and its figure varies that setting: the centre

\subsection{Cambourne: a new settlement and the four outputs}

Cambourne is a fast-growing Cambridgeshire new settlement; the
15{,}000 target is hypothetical and reflects no assessment of
local housing need. This reference case presents the four outputs
(Table~\ref{tab:fouroutputs}), varies the centre walk, and ends with
the transit demonstration, a proposed
corridor and hub. Figure~\ref{fig:cambourne}
shows the selected plan under the
baseline and 1{,}600~m centre walks. At the baseline 1{,}200~m
walk, the plan accommodates 15{,}020 new residents against a
15{,}000 target, and they average a 273~m walk to a centre and
116~m to green space. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} sweeps the walk.

Table~\ref{tab:fouroutputs} reproduces the Cambourne scenario's run
report, which records the four outputs. Pruning takes 477 of the
15{,}497 people grown, and the three options house the same
15{,}020 on the shared fabric, differing only in their centres. The
raw grown state carries 26 centre areas, of which 20 are the
existing centres the window already has and six were earned by the
new residents; as grown and optimised placement add two more where
pruning left growth without provision within the walk, and fewest
centres removes two. The walk falls from 557~m in the raw grown
state to 343~m once the centres are sized to the residents they
serve, and to 273~m once each centre is moved central to the homes
it serves. Fewest centres trades some of that back, 292~m for two
centres fewer.

A final run adds proposed transit, which the preceding runs did not
use. We draw a proposed bus
corridor from Cambourne's eastern edge across the plain to the
window's north-eastern village and add a proposed bus rapid transit
stop as a transit hub at the corridor's Cambourne end, beside the
A428 junction.
The corridor preference is 0.95 and the other settings stay at the
baseline. Growth and its satellite settlements string along the route
instead of spreading across the window (Figure~\ref{fig:corridor}),
and the share of new homes within the proposed route's catchments
rises from 10\% to 86\%. The hub lies just beyond the centre walk of the
existing centres, so its catchment holds land they do not serve, and a new
settlement gathers around its pinned centre at the junction. The
population accommodated moves little, 15{,}076 against 15{,}020,
because the catchments hold enough developable land for a
settlement of this size; only its location changes. The mean
centre walk lengthens from 273 to 348~m, since growth strung along
a route is served by centres spaced along it rather than gathered
around a core, while the walk to green space rises from 116 to
127~m. The walk constraint holds nonetheless.

\subsection{Crews Hill: a green-belt release}

The Crews Hill area of Enfield, inside the M25 at London's northern edge,
is one of the largest green-belt releases proposed in an emerging London
local plan, which allocates about 5{,}500 homes around an existing rail
station. We built the scenario to sketch the walkable settlement the
release could hold and the green network that would survive. Growth must
stay attached, so the extension is contiguous. The density tiers use
indicative UK design-code values (110 / 65 / 40 dwellings per hectare,
high to low, at a household size of 2.36). The scenario takes from the emerging plan only
the site boundary and the allocation's scale. Whether the release
should proceed is a question for the plan's examination and its
public consultation.

Under the baseline settings the plan houses 13{,}193 new residents
against a 13{,}000 target, and they
average a 137~m walk to a centre and 60~m to green space. Two-thirds of
them are within the transit catchments, the 400~m stop and
1{,}200~m hub walks, since growth leans
toward the transit the window already has.
Figure~\ref{fig:crewshill} varies the shares on the fixed boundary.
Because the release can hold the target at any of the three share
settings,
the shares set the land it takes rather than the population: 0.5 /
0.3 / 0.2 (high / medium / low) houses 13{,}123 on 67~ha of new
land, and 0.1 / 0.3 / 0.6 houses 13{,}128 on 101~ha, half as much
again for the same people. The
station's hub centre is pinned against the rail line, and the
new development forms around the station.

\subsection{Pajarito, Medell\'in: growth shaped by terrain}

Pajarito and Ciudadela Nuevo Occidente on Medell\'in's north-western
slopes form a planned expansion of high-rise social housing served by
the Metrocable. Slopes over 20 degrees are unbuildable and cover about 30\% of
the study window, so the green network and the short walking distances
must fit around the terrain. The high density tier (35{,}000 people per
km\(^2\)) reflects the area's housing towers. The committed scenario
supplies bus stops but no stations, so growth leans toward the stops
and no centre is pinned at a hub. The scenario is a
hypothetical demonstration on the district's terrain and carries no
recommendation for its future development.

At the baseline walk the plan accommodates 39{,}597 against the
40{,}000 target with a 102~m mean
centre walk for new homes; the steep slopes fragment the buildable
land into detached benches of gentler ground.
Figure~\ref{fig:medellin} varies the two settings that bind here.
Requiring growth to stay attached to existing development collapses
the plan to 5{,}025, because a centre may then only be founded
against fabric that already exists, and the benches are unreachable
across the steep ground between them. Raising the service viability
threshold from 2{,}000 to 4{,}000 people houses 40{,}037 in fewer
and larger settlements, at a 176~m mean centre walk.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the centre walk}

The centre walk is swept across 800, 1{,}200, and 1{,}600~m for the
three cases. The population accommodated moves little across the
sweep: Cambourne houses 15{,}020 to 15{,}205, Crews Hill 13{,}035
to 13{,}193, and Pajarito 39{,}157 to 39{,}949. The walk instead
changes how many centres serve those residents and how far they
walk to one.

A centre is founded once the population nearest it reaches the
service viability threshold, so a wider walk lets a centre gather that
population from more land, and fewer centres are founded. Crews
Hill shows the effect, at 82~m under an 800~m walk against 137~m
at the baseline and 293~m at 1{,}600~m. The mean walk need not rise
with the setting, because the walk also bounds how far a home may sit
from its centre, and the two effects meet differently on each
window's land. Each value comes from one selected run, and the
setting changes which run is selected.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Effects of the adaptations}

Real terrain changes the meaning of the walkability guarantee. On
the abstract plain the guarantee follows from the rules, but on
real inputs it becomes a property of the resulting plan and can
be checked against the map. Results here do not compare directly
with published abstract-plain morphologies, because the walk metric,
the green-space rules, and the seeding rules differ, as
Table~\ref{tab:rules} records.

The shared bounded grid walk for growth and scoring is the largest
departure. A street-network metric is common in walkability
analysis, and earlier releases used one over the existing fabric
with grid walks over new growth, which has no streets until a later
design stage. We removed the hybrid because a distance measured
partly over streets and partly over the grid has no consistent
meaning. The bounded grid walk with carved barriers treats new and
existing fabric on the same terms and does not cross motorways,
railways, or rivers.

\subsection{What post-processing contributes}

The published model leaves a centrality where it seeded and leaves each
block at the density it drew. Both are reasonable on an abstract plain,
where a centrality seeds in open country and the settlement grows around
it symmetrically. On real terrain neither holds, and the three
post-processing steps of Section~\ref{sec:fouroutputs} address what
breaks.

Placement is the clearest gain. A centre is founded at the edge of the
development that earns it, because that is where growth has reached,
and the settlement then grows past it. At the Cambourne baseline the new
homes average a 343~m walk to the centres where the run left them and
273~m once each is moved to the point central to the homes it serves.
That step changes no fabric: it moves the centres and shortens the mean
walk by 70~m. The raw grown state's 557~m is not the
comparison, since its centres are single cells that post-processing has
yet to size.

Viability is the second. The growth rules found a centre only where the
population justifies one, so the pass has little to remove at the
Cambourne baseline, 477 of the 15{,}497 residents grown, and it
works mainly as an audit. The run report records the surviving
centres against the threshold; separately, each post-processing
edit enforces that no plan option leaves a home beyond the centre
walk of a centre.

Two details of the accounting matter for reading the centre counts. A
centre passes the viability test on the new homes within its centre
walk, and these areas overlap, so neighbouring settlements pool the
demand that keeps their centres viable. The counts also include the
centres the window already has: 20 of the Cambourne raw grown
state's 26 areas are
existing centres, which serve the existing town and hold no account
toward founding new ones, so new growth earned the other six itself.

Density arrangement is the third, and its contribution is
presentational rather than modelling. The growth rules draw a tier for each cell
from the shares, which fixes the mix and therefore the population, but
they draw it when the cell is built, before the settlement that will
surround it exists. Arranging those drawn values by the walk to the middle
of the nearest centre produces the density gradient a plan drawing is
expected to show, and because the tiers are dealt from the same shares
over the same cells, the shares and the population are unchanged.

The boundary between growth and post-processing matters for how the
outputs are read. Growth produces
the raw grown state; post-processing improves how that state is served and
presented, and apart from pruning unserved growth it adds no
development. The raw layer is written with each run so the two can be
compared directly.

\subsection{What the cases separate}

The three windows answer to different settings, and each isolates a
different effect. At Crews Hill the release holds its population under
the share settings tried, so the shares set the land it takes rather than
the population it houses: the densest shares build on 67~ha of new land
and the lightest on 101~ha. Where
a boundary is fixed by policy, as a green-belt release is, the shares
govern that trade.

Pajarito is the case where a setting decides the outcome rather than
shading it. Requiring growth to stay attached to existing development
houses 5{,}025 against 39{,}597 when detached settlements are allowed,
because the benches of gentler ground are separated by slopes over 20
degrees and a centre may only be founded on land growth can reach.
Whether that difference is a finding or an artefact depends on what the
attachment setting is taken to mean. The setting concerns
development rather than movement: the model does not ask whether a road
or a bus could span the steep ground between two benches, only whether
built fabric runs continuously across it. Read that way, the result
follows from the setting, and a planner who intends the benches to be
developed, reached by roads or transit the model does not represent,
sets the model to allow detached settlements.

Cambourne, by contrast, houses much the same population across the
settings swept; the settings move the walk instead. This supports
reading these outputs as sketches of arrangement rather than as
capacity studies.

\subsection{Limitations}

The outputs are sketches of spatial arrangement, and the model omits
much of what turns an arrangement into a plan: land ownership and
assembly, development viability, phasing, infrastructure cost, and
tenure. A sketch shows where a walkable settlement could sit, and
says nothing about whether its land can be brought forward. The
inputs carry the limits of their sources. OpenStreetMap coverage
varies by place; at Pajarito the informal fabric is likely to be
under-mapped, so the existing development the model keeps fixed is
less complete there than in the UK cases. The slope bands stand in
for buildability rather than for geological hazard. The
demonstrations are hypothetical throughout: no case has been
evaluated with the planners and residents the tool is intended to
serve. That evaluation belongs to applied work with a live plan, and
until then the tool remains research software whose outputs are
material for discussion rather than plans to build from.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This implementation builds on the published model and turns Isobenefit
Urbanism into an instrument a planner can run on a real place and
steer through its settings. It
keeps the published growth rules recognisable and adapts them to real
geography through the departures that Table~\ref{tab:rules} records. The
plan-derivation pipeline scores each run on walkable access, keeps
the best single run, and derives the four outputs, with the walk
constraint enforced in the plan options. The three case studies each
answer to a different setting: at Cambourne the centre walk
sets how far a home stands from its centre, at Crews Hill the density
shares set how much of the release is built on, and at Pajarito the
terrain makes the choice between attached and detached settlements
decisive. The Cambourne corridor demonstration adds the transit
settings, which draw growth to a proposed route and leave the
population it houses much as it was. The
code, scenario data, and parameters are open,
and the results rebuild from committed sources at fixed seeds.

The case studies are hypothetical demonstrations, and a live
application would begin in consultation with local residents and
planners.
