\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Isobenefit Urbanism proposes settlements in which every home lies
within a walk of a mixed-use centre and of green space. The published
model grows such settlements on an abstract plain, where a few rules
guarantee the walks while the urban form emerges. Real places arrive
with existing towns, fragmented green space, rivers, railways,
motorways, and steep slopes, and with the transit around which policy
already organises growth. This paper adapts the model to real terrain
and implements it as an open-source QGIS plugin, so that the rules
become an instrument for steering development on an actual site. The
plugin prepares a scenario from OpenStreetMap and elevation data, or
from the user's own layers, keeps existing settlements as they stand,
and simulates growth within the land released for development. The
planner steers the outcome through the scenario settings: the walking
distances, the population target for new residents, the density tiers
and their shares, the land withheld from development, and the
existing or proposed transit toward which growth is drawn. The result
is a set of plan options written as GIS layers, in which every new
home keeps a mixed-use centre and green space within the chosen
walks. Three hypothetical cases demonstrate the pipeline: a new
settlement at Cambourne, a green-belt release at Crews Hill in
London, and a hillside expansion at Pajarito in Medell\'in, each
shaped by a different dominant constraint. The outputs are scenario
sketches for discussion and feedback at the early stages of planning.
All code, scenario data, and parameters are open, and every result
rebuilds from committed sources at fixed seeds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Isobenefit Urbanism \citep{dacci2019} proposes that the benefits of a
settlement should be distributed evenly. Its prefix \emph{iso} means
equal: wherever one lives, a local centre with shops, services, and
workplaces should be within a walk, and so should green space.
\citet{dacci2013} introduced the concept in a futures study and later
formalised it as a cellular-automaton morphogenesis, published
with a Python simulator \citep{dacci_voto2023,voto_code}. The automaton
grows a grid of land cells from seeded centralities (the published
model's term for centres) under a small set of rules that maintain
the walking limits as the settlement grows. The approach sits between master-planning and
laissez-faire growth, because a few simple rules guide development while
the detailed form emerges spontaneously \citep{dacci_banister_white2024}.

The published model runs on an abstract grid, in which every cell
starts as nature, distances are straight lines, and a single radius,
typically about a fifteen-minute walk, governs access both to
centres and to green space. This abstraction keeps the morphology of the
rules easy to study. On a real site the model meets existing towns,
fragmented green space, rivers, railways, motorways, and steep
slopes, together with the transit around which policy already
organises growth; the walkable-access premise of Isobenefit Urbanism is shared
by transit-oriented development, which holds that growth and density
belong within walking reach of transit \citep{calthorpe1993,
cervero_kockelman1997, ibraeva2020}. Carried onto such a site, the
rules can steer where development goes, provided the model respects
what stands and returns its results in the formats planning practice
uses.

This paper operationalises Isobenefit Urbanism as a QGIS plugin, an
instrument planning workflows can incorporate for conceptualising
options and consulting residents and experts
\citep[cf.][]{waddell2002}. The plugin prepares the inputs, keeps
existing settlements as they stand, and grows new development within
the land a scenario releases. The planner steers the outcome through
the scenario settings: the two walking distances, the population
target for new residents, the density tiers and their shares, the
land withheld from development, and the existing or proposed transit
toward which growth is drawn. The results are post-processed into
plan options and written as GIS layers.

The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item An adaptation of the published rules to real geography. The
  model keeps existing settlements and protected green space as they
  stand, simulates growth within land released for development, and
  measures access as walks that detour around barriers and terrain.
  Departures from the published model are tabulated
  (Table~\ref{tab:rules}).
\item An open, reproducible implementation: a QGIS plugin that wraps a
  simulation engine published on PyPI, with documentation and the
  case-study scenarios.
\item Enhancements beyond the published model. The single radius is
  separated into two walking distances, one to a centre and one to
  green space, each with its own rules. Transit-oriented development
  enters as an explicit heuristic: transit hubs anchor centres, and a
  corridor preference draws growth toward existing or proposed
  transit. Post-processing optimises the placement of centres and
  grades the densities from centre to edge, and the plan options keep
  every new home within the centre walk.
\end{enumerate}

We demonstrate the pipeline on three case studies, each dominated by a
different constraint and each steered through a different setting.
Sections~\ref{sec:background} to~\ref{sec:discussion} cover the background,
the methods, the case studies, and the discussion.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The published model}

The Isobenefit-cities simulator \citep{dacci_voto2023} belongs to a
long line of cellular models of urban form with real-world planning
applications \citep{white_engelen1993,
batty2005, sante2010}, and differs from models fitted to observed
growth in that its rules enforce a walkability guarantee while growth
proceeds. It grows settlements on a
square grid in which every cell starts as nature. Each simulation step scans
the grid against an unchanged copy. A nature cell adjacent to built land may
become built on two conditions: a centrality must lie within the
radius T\(^*\), typically about one kilometre or a fifteen-minute
walk, and building the cell must not breach the green-space rules,
which keep nature as one connected region whose width does not fall
below T\(^*\). An eligible cell builds by a random draw at each
step, so the built edge advances irregularly. New
centralities seed by rarer draws, either beside built land that has
outgrown its centres or on open land. Each built block takes its
population density from one of three tiers, high, medium, or low,
drawn at set shares, and the run stops when the total population
reaches its cap.

\subsection{Walking distances and standards}

We replace the single radius with two walking distances, one to a
centre and one to green space. Both are set in the QGIS plugin, whose
defaults of 1{,}200~m for the centre walk and 400~m for the
green walk are approximately fifteen-minute and five-minute walks
respectively. The walkable-city literature generally treats
destinations within a ten-to-fifteen-minute walk as walkable
\citep{moreno2021, abbiasov2024}, and observed walking distances for
everyday errands are of the same order \citep{yang_diezroux2012}. For
green space, the WHO Regional Office for Europe advises everyday access
within about 300~m straight-line distance \citep{who2016}, and Natural
England's accessible natural greenspace standard uses the same figure
\citep{naturalengland2010}. Both standards also set a minimum size
for qualifying green space: the WHO advice concerns spaces of at
least half a hectare to one hectare, and the Natural England standard
applies from 2 hectares, which is the default for the
minimum park area (Section~\ref{sec:growth}). The 400~m default is slightly
longer than the advised 300~m because a walking route goes around
obstacles and covers more distance than the straight line.
Transit adds two further distances (Section~\ref{sec:transit}). The
stop catchment, 400~m by default, bounds the walk to a bus stop or a
drawn transit route, and matches the quarter-mile bus-stop catchment
conventional in transit-oriented development \citep{calthorpe1993,
ibraeva2020}. The hub catchment, 1{,}200~m by default, bounds the
walk to a station or a designated hub. Station catchments in the same
practice start at half a mile, and the default matches the centre
walk, because a hub anchors a centre.
Section~\ref{sec:cases} sweeps the centre walk across 800, 1{,}200,
and 1{,}600~m; the green walk stays at 400~m throughout.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The QGIS plugin comprises two tools, one for data preparation and one
for simulation. The preparation tool downloads a scenario's input
layers from OpenStreetMap. Users can instead supply equivalent
layers from their own data, or download the layers and edit them.
The simulation tool passes these inputs to the
engine, published on PyPI, which applies the growth rules and the
post-processing. The subsections below follow that sequence.

\subsection{Real inputs}
\label{sec:inputs}

Each scenario is defined by a simulation boundary in a metric coordinate
reference system and a set of vector layers. The case-study scenarios
fetch these layers from OpenStreetMap
\citep{osm} with the preparation tool. The layers cover existing
built fabric, protected green space, existing mixed-use centres,
unbuildable land (the areas treated as unavailable for development),
streets, public-transport stops, rail and tram stations,
railways, and industrial land, which counts as unbuildable for new
homes. Terrain slope is mapped from the Copernicus GLO-30 elevation model
\citep{copernicus_glo30} as bands steeper than 15, 20, 25, and 30
degrees. The bands are a separate layer that users can edit from
local knowledge.

\subsection{Grid preparation}
\label{sec:gridprep}

The simulation tool rasterises the input layers to a working grid at a
user-selected cell size, such as 50~m, though a finer grid may be
preferable where the extent and the available computation allow. Each
cell takes its class from the layer that intersects it:
existing built fabric, green space, or unbuildable land. A cell that
intersects none of the layers is marked open land. The tool then adds
to the unbuildable class in two ways. It carves corridors along
motorways, trunk roads, railways, and rivers, and it marks the slope
bands that lie above the scenario's slope limit. The remaining street
geometry is kept for display only.

The model treats new development and the existing fabric differently.
The existing fabric stays as mapped, and the model does not redevelop it,
for two reasons. First, the emphasis is on new development, so the
population target and the walking distances concern new homes, and
the constraints in the sections that follow are derived from and
applied to the new growth. Second, the input data rarely describe
existing densities and services in sufficient detail to model them.

The simulation tool then screens the open land against the service
viability threshold, the population a minimal local centre needs;
the default of 2{,}000 people sits at the small end of published
facility catchments \citep{barton2021}. A region too small to hold
the threshold population is kept when it lies within the centre
walk of an existing centre, where new growth is infill served by
that centre. The rest, typically small islands and slivers of open
land enclosed by existing development or unbuildable corridors, is
excluded from new development before the run.

The growth rules and all reported metrics measure
distance with the bounded grid walk: the shortest path over
traversable cells, which detours around unbuildable land and is cut
off at the maximum walking distance. The path is computed by
Dijkstra search.

\subsection{The growth rules}
\label{sec:growth}

A run proceeds in iterations. Each iteration applies the growth
rules to every land cell, visiting the cells in a shuffled order to
avoid the directional bias of a fixed scan. A cell is a candidate
for building when it is empty, buildable, touches built land, and
lies within the centre walk of a centre.
New growth attaches only to built land that carries a centre, so
existing settlements without one, such as outlying farmsteads and
hamlets, receive no new growth. A run starts with the existing mixed-use
centres and with a centre seeded at each transit hub, a rail or tram
station or a point the planner designates, and development gathers
around the hubs from the first iteration.

Building a candidate must leave the green network intact. A park is
a contiguous green area of at least the minimum park area, 2
hectares by default, which follows Natural England's accessible
natural greenspace standard \citep{naturalengland2010}. New
development may not shrink a park below the minimum, whether by
splitting it or by building along its edge, and a green corridor
between developments may not narrow below a minimum span. Every
built cell, the existing fabric included, must keep a park within
the green walk.

A candidate that passes every check builds by a random draw at the
build probability. The draw staggers the built edge, and the
probability sets the pace of growth. Each built cell draws one of the three density tiers
according to the shares, and a run stops when it reaches the
population target.

New centres arise in two ways. Where a cell at the growing edge lies
beyond the centre walk of every existing and grown centre, the
settlement has outgrown its centres, and that cell may become a new
centre and seed development around it. The conversion is a random
draw at the seeding probability, limited to one new centre per
iteration, and seeding stops once the run reaches 80\% of the
population target. Separately, a dispersal probability allows
leapfrogging: by a per-cell draw, a new centre may seed on open land
away from existing development and begin a satellite settlement.
Setting the probability to zero disables leapfrogging.

Transit also enters the draws. Transit corridors, mapped from bus
stops or drawn as proposed routes, project the stop catchment via the
grid walk, and the hubs project the wider hub catchment in the same
way. Outside both catchments the build, seeding, and dispersal draws
are scaled by one minus the corridor preference, a weight from 0 to 1
(Section~\ref{sec:transit}). At the default of 0 the draws are
unchanged, and higher settings concentrate growth and satellite
settlements along existing or proposed transit.

\subsection{Ensembles and selection}
\label{sec:ensemble}

The growth rules are stochastic, so a single run is one pattern
among the many the same inputs allow. The tool therefore runs an
ensemble, fifty runs in the scenarios reported here. Each run
starts from the same inputs with its own random seed and grows a
complete settlement pattern that satisfies the constraints, so
runs build in different places. The likelihood layers
summarise this variation by recording, for each cell, the fraction
of runs in which it ended built or green, which indicates how
likely an area is to develop under the constraints.

Because the runs vary cell by cell, averaging them blends the
constraints away; the blended result no longer satisfies the walking
limits, so the layers are informational only. We instead select the
best single run, the one whose candidate plan from the
post-processing of Section~\ref{sec:fouroutputs} scores lowest.
The score averages the new homes' walks to a centre and to green
space, counting a home beyond either walk at twice that walk's
limit and any unhoused remainder of the population target as
homes beyond both walks, so a run cannot score well by housing
fewer people.

A reported mean walk averages over the new homes that reach a
centre or green space within the walk.

\subsection{Post-processing and the four outputs}
\label{sec:fouroutputs}

Post-processing checks each run before a plan is drawn.
The first check is viability: each centre, repositioned central to
the homes it serves, must gather at least the threshold population
of new homes within the centre walk. A centre that falls short is
removed, and any growth left without a centre within the walk is
pruned back to nature, an outcome the three plan options share. Existing centres, the hub centres,
and the existing fabric are unaffected;
pruning is the only step that removes growth, and the run
report confirms that every surviving centre meets the threshold.

Post-processing then redistributes the drawn densities within each
settlement, with the highest tiers at the core and lower tiers
toward the edge; because the values are only redistributed, the
run's shares and its population do not change.

The last step produces the three plan options, which
together with the raw grown state form the four outputs. \emph{Raw}
shows the best single run as it grew, coloured by the density
tiers it drew. The three options share the same fabric and
arranged density and differ only in their centres. \emph{As grown} keeps the run's
centres, adding one only where pruning removed a cluster's serving
centre. \emph{Optimised placement} keeps the repositioned centres
and adds one wherever provision is missing;
infill too small to meet the threshold is served by existing
centres and receives no centre of its own. \emph{Fewest centres}
removes centres one at a time for as long as every home keeps one
within the walk. Every option enforces the walk constraint: no edit
may leave a new home beyond the centre walk of a centre. Optimised
placement is the selected plan; run selection scores each run's
optimised-placement candidate, and the case studies report that
option.

A centre is a mixed-use district whose cells carry homes at the
arranged densities alongside floor area for services. Each centre
is sized by the new residents nearest to it, at 20~m\(^2\) of
centre area per resident by default, and each resident counts
toward one centre, so total provision follows the population. The
default covers the shops, services, and workplaces of
neighbourhood-centre standards \citep{barton2021}. Because amenity
provision scales sublinearly with settlement size
\citep{bettencourt2013}, a single ratio suits the small-to-mid
settlement growth the tool addresses.

An ensemble run loads its results into the QGIS project as one
layer group: the existing fabric for comparison,
the raw grown state, and the three plan options as categorical
rasters, each coloured by density tier with the centres in a
distinct hue and the centre count in the layer name. A further
layer maps the growth that pruning removed, and the two likelihood
layers complete the group. Alongside the layers the tool writes a
plain-text run report listing the parameters, the run summary,
the four outputs' metrics side by side, and the achieved shares
and centre audit, so every post-processing edit stays visible. A
single run, outside the ensemble, is written instead as a
temporal animation of the growth, one frame per step.

\subsection{Transit-oriented growth}
\label{sec:transit}

Transit enters the model through the hubs and the corridors.
Each transit hub seeds a centre at the start of a
run, so development gathers around it, and post-processing pins the
centre, which is neither pruned nor repositioned and is sized like
any other. A point added to the hubs layer places
a designated centre by hand, such as a transit-oriented development
around a planned station. Transit corridors attract growth. The
corridor preference of Section~\ref{sec:growth} scales the
development draws outside the transit catchments, so a preference
above 0 concentrates growth and satellite settlements along existing
or proposed transit. The run report gives the share of new homes
within the transit catchments and the mean walk to the nearest
transit stop or hub. These metrics
do not enter run selection. The baseline case studies
leave the preference at 0, so their reported numbers do not depend on
the corridor mechanism. A Cambourne demonstration raises the
preference to 0.95 (Section~\ref{sec:cases}).

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}
\label{sec:software}

The implementation comprises a simulation engine and a QGIS plugin.
The engine is a Rust crate, published on PyPI as
\texttt{isobenefit}, whose small Python interface, built with PyO3,
accepts and returns arrays; the engine does not import QGIS. The
QGIS plugin \citep{qgis} supplies the data preparation, the run
dialog, and the post-processing and reporting, and depends only on
what QGIS already bundles plus the engine. The QGIS plugin repository does not permit
shipping binaries, so the compiled engine installs from PyPI and the
plugin offers to install it on first run.

Results are deterministic and independent of thread count, because
every stochastic draw derives from its own fixed seed and parallel
totals are integer sums whose order does not matter. The test suite
cross-checks the Rust engine against a reference Python
implementation of the bounded grid walk and of the labelling of
contiguous regions. A single verification script runs the same
checks as the continuous-integration pipeline (formatting, linting,
Rust and Python tests, and the website build); releases are tagged,
version-locked between engine and plugin, and published
automatically. All scenario inputs are committed, so every result
in this paper rebuilds offline from source at fixed seeds.

\subsection{Cambourne: a new settlement and the four outputs}

Cambourne is a fast-growing Cambridgeshire new settlement. The
15{,}000 target is hypothetical and reflects no assessment of
local housing need. This reference case presents the four outputs
(Table~\ref{tab:fouroutputs}), varies the centre walk, and ends with
the transit demonstration, a proposed
corridor and hub. Figure~\ref{fig:cambourne}
shows the selected plan under the
baseline and 1{,}600~m centre walks. At the baseline 1{,}200~m
walk, the plan accommodates 4{,}546 of the 15{,}000 target,
and new homes average a 264~m walk to a centre and 92~m to green
space. At 1{,}600~m the wider catchments gather more new homes
toward the threshold, and the sweep of
Table~\ref{tab:sweep} quantifies the response.

Table~\ref{tab:fouroutputs} reproduces the Cambourne scenario's run
report, which records the four outputs. The raw grown
state approaches the target (14{,}592 of 15{,}000), but much of
that growth lies in dispersed pockets that hold too few new homes
to meet the 2{,}000-person threshold. Pruning removes them, they
revert to nature, and the plan options retain 30\% of the target.
The three plan options coincide in this case: each keeps 23 centre
areas and the same population, and their mean centre walks agree
within 1~m, so the contrast lies between the raw grown state and
the plans rather than among the options.

A final run exercises both transit levers. We draw a proposed bus
corridor from Cambourne's eastern edge across the plain to the
window's north-eastern village and add a proposed bus rapid transit
stop as a transit hub at its southern end beside the A428 junction.
The corridor preference is 0.95 and every other setting stays at the
baseline. Growth and its satellite settlements string along the route
instead of spreading across the window (Figure~\ref{fig:corridor}),
and the share of new homes within the transit catchments rises from
16\% to 78\%. The hub lies just beyond the centre walk of the
existing centres, so its catchment holds land they do not serve, and a new
settlement gathers around its pinned centre at the junction. The
population accommodated rises from 4{,}546 to 12{,}155: draws
outside the catchments run at a twentieth of their probability, so
the growth that the baseline scatters into sub-threshold pockets
gathers instead into fewer and larger settlements along the route,
and these meet the threshold and survive pruning. The mean walks
concede little (304~m against 264~m to a centre, 113~m against 92~m
to green space), and every home keeps both walks. In this window the
corridor preference aligns growth with transit and, by concentrating
it, raises the population the plan retains.

\subsection{Crews Hill: a green-belt release}

The Crews Hill area of Enfield, inside the M25 at London's northern edge,
is one of the largest green-belt releases proposed in an emerging London
local plan, which allocates about 5{,}500 homes around an existing rail
station. We built the scenario to sketch the walkable settlement the
release could hold and the green network that survives. Dispersal
is off, so the extension grows contiguously. The density tiers use
indicative UK design-code values (40 / 65 / 110 dwellings per hectare at a
household size of 2.36). The scenario takes from the emerging plan only
the site boundary and the allocation's scale. Whether the release
should proceed is a question for the plan's examination and its
public consultation.

Under the baseline settings the plan reaches 10{,}428 of the 13{,}000
target, and new homes
average a 145~m walk to a centre and 140~m to green space.
Figure~\ref{fig:crewshill} varies the shares on the fixed boundary.
The growth rules bound the reachable extent of the release, and every
setting builds on close to the same
4{,}300 cells, so the shares alone set the population. Shares of
0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2 (high / medium / low) reach 12{,}952, near the
target, and shares of 0.1 / 0.3 / 0.6 house 8{,}539. The
station's hub centre is pinned against the rail line, and the
new development forms around the station.

\subsection{Pajarito, Medell\'in: topography as the dominant constraint}

Pajarito and Ciudadela Nuevo Occidente on Medell\'in's northwestern slopes
are a planned expansion of high-rise social housing served by the
Metrocable. Slopes over 20 degrees are unbuildable and cover about 30\% of
the study window, so the green network and the short walking distances
must fit around the terrain. The high density tier (35{,}000 people per
km\(^2\)) reflects the area's housing towers. The scenario is a
hypothetical demonstration on the district's terrain and carries no
recommendation for its future development.

At the baseline walk the plan accommodates about 33{,}600 of the 40{,}000
target with a 51~m mean
centre walk for new homes; the steep slopes fragment the buildable
land into detached benches of gentler ground. Figure~\ref{fig:medellin} varies
dispersal. Aggressive dispersal matches the moderate baseline
(33{,}630 against 33{,}617 people), while turning dispersal off
collapses the plan to 2{,}651, because dispersal seeding is the only
mechanism that reaches the benches. The satellites it seeds meet
the threshold by gathering new homes across the intervening green
space.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the centre walk}

The centre walk is swept across 800, 1{,}200, and 1{,}600~m for the
three cases. The centre walk sets the size of a centre's catchment,
and a wider catchment gathers more new homes toward the threshold.
The walk therefore also governs which centres are viable. Cambourne, where
viability limits the baseline plan, responds most, with the
population accommodated rising from 2{,}900 at 800~m to 4{,}500
at 1{,}200~m and 10{,}500 at 1{,}600~m. At Cambourne's greenfield
densities an 800~m catchment holds too few new homes to sustain a
centre, so the 2{,}000-person threshold rules out many of the study
window's detached mid-sized pockets. Crews Hill holds near
10{,}400 at every setting, because its limit is the reachable
extent of the release rather than the walk, and Pajarito climbs
from 30{,}800 to 35{,}400.

which responds less regularly, because each figure comes from one

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Effects of the adaptations}

Real terrain changes the meaning of the walkability guarantee. On
the abstract plain the guarantee follows from the rules, but on
real inputs it becomes a property of the resulting plan and can
be checked against the map. Results here do not compare directly
with published abstract-plain morphologies, because the walk metric,
the green-space rules, and the seeding rules differ, as
Table~\ref{tab:rules} records.

The shared bounded grid walk for growth and scoring is the largest
departure. A street-network metric is common in walkability
analysis, and earlier releases used one over the existing fabric
with grid walks over new growth, which has no streets until a later
design stage. We removed the hybrid because a distance measured
partly over streets and partly over the grid has no consistent
meaning. The bounded grid walk with carved barriers treats new and
existing fabric on the same terms and does not cross motorways,
railways, or rivers.

\subsection{The post-processing gap}

The gap between the raw grown state and the plan options (97\%
against 30\% of target at the Cambourne baseline) is produced by
pruning alone. The pruned growth lies in pockets that held too few
new homes to meet the 2{,}000-person threshold when the run
approached its target, and a plan that kept them as free-standing
settlements would promise centres their populations cannot sustain.
A threshold that removes two thirds of the
grown population at the baseline walk indicates that the permitted
walk, the dispersal setting, and the threshold interact
strongly, so the threshold warrants per-scenario scrutiny rather
than a single default. The Cambourne transit demonstration shows
one lever against the loss: concentrating growth along a corridor
gathers it into settlements that meet the threshold, and the plan
retains 81\% of the target against the baseline's 30\%.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This implementation builds on the published model and turns Isobenefit
Urbanism into an instrument a planner can run on a real place and
steer through its settings. It
keeps the published growth rules recognisable and adapts them to real
geography through the departures that Table~\ref{tab:rules} records. The
plan-derivation pipeline scores every run on walkable access, keeps
the best single run, and derives the four outputs, with the walk
constraint enforced in every option. In the three case studies the
outcome follows the dominant constraint: viability at Cambourne, the
reachable extent of the release at Crews Hill, and terrain at
Pajarito. The transit levers place transit-oriented development in
the same pipeline, and the Cambourne corridor demonstration shows
them steering growth to a proposed route while retaining more of
the target. The
software, data, and parameters are open,
and the results rebuild from committed sources at fixed seeds.

The case studies are hypothetical demonstrations, and a live
application would begin in consultation with local residents and
planners.
